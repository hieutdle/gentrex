Appendix: Test Methodology and Limitations

Genomic DNA is isolated from the submitted specimen and checked for quantity and quality before testing begins. Specimens that fail these checks are not tested, and the ordering clinician is asked for a new specimen. All testing is performed at {{ lab.name }}, {{ details.address }}.

Regions of interest are enriched with hybridization probes and sequenced on a massively parallel sequencing instrument. Reads are aligned to the human reference genome, and differences from the reference are identified with validated software. The analysis covers the coding exons of the targeted genes and the adjacent intronic bases. A region that does not reach the minimum read depth is completed with an orthogonal method where this is technically possible.

Findings that meet the laboratory's reporting criteria are confirmed with a second method when the confirmation policy requires it. The interpretation follows the joint recommendations of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology, and draws on population databases, the published literature and the laboratory's own data.

Limitations

No genetic test detects every possible change. This assay may not detect changes in regions that were not targeted, large rearrangements, changes in regions with high sequence similarity to other parts of the genome, low-level mosaicism, or changes in the number of short tandem repeats, unless the test description names them. Rare differences under a probe or primer can prevent a region from being read.

A result reflects the knowledge available on the day of the report. The laboratory does not reanalyze data routinely. A clinician may request a reanalysis when new clinical information becomes available. Results must be interpreted together with the clinical findings, the family history and other laboratory data. Errors in the labeling of a specimen, a bone marrow transplant from a donor, or a recent blood transfusion can lead to an incorrect result.

This test was developed and its performance characteristics were determined by {{ lab.name }}. It has not been cleared or approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration.

Information for Patients and Families

This page explains in general terms how to read a genetic test report. It is not about your own result. Please talk to your doctor or a genetic counselor about what your result means for you.

What a genetic test looks for

Genes are instructions that the body uses to grow and to work. Everyone has small differences in their genes, and most of them have no effect on health. A genetic test reads some or all of a person's genes and looks for differences that are known or suspected to cause a medical condition.

What the result can and cannot tell you

A report can describe a difference that explains a person's symptoms, a difference whose meaning is not yet understood, or no reportable difference at all. A report without a finding lowers the chance that a condition has a genetic cause, but it does not rule one out, because no test examines everything. The meaning of a difference can also change as researchers learn more.

What it can mean for relatives

Family members share part of their genes. A finding in one person can therefore matter for parents, brothers, sisters and children. Whether relatives should be tested, and when, depends on the condition and on each person's wishes. A genetic counselor can help a family decide who might want testing and how to talk about it.

Privacy

Genetic information is protected health information. In the United States, the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act limits how health insurers and employers may use it. The law does not cover life insurance, disability insurance or long-term care insurance. You may wish to ask about these before you share a result.

Questions

Our client services team cannot give medical advice, but it can help your clinician reach one of our genetic counselors. Clinicians can call {{ details.toll_free }} on weekdays.

Family testing

When a report describes a finding that can be passed on in a family, relatives can usually be tested for that one finding. The ordering clinician can request a family testing form from client services. The form asks for the accession number of the original report, so please keep this report with the patient's records.

Reclassification

The laboratory reviews the evidence for its classifications over time. When the classification of a reported finding changes in a way that could affect care, an amended report is sent to the ordering clinician on record. Please tell us when the patient's clinician changes.

Data sharing

To help other laboratories and researchers, {{ lab.name }} submits de-identified findings and their classifications to public databases. No names, dates of birth or record numbers are shared. Patients who do not want their de-identified findings shared can opt out through their clinician.

Billing and Insurance Information

This page is for the patient's records. It is not a bill. A statement is sent separately once the claim has been processed.

Insurance

{{ lab.name }} bills the insurance plan on file for the test. The plan decides how much it pays and how much the patient owes, according to the terms of the policy. Some plans ask for prior authorization. When a plan denies a claim, the laboratory files an appeal on the patient's behalf where the plan allows it, and may ask the ordering clinician for a letter of medical necessity.

Explanation of benefits

After a claim is processed, the insurance plan sends an explanation of benefits. This document lists the amount that was billed and the amount that the plan paid. It often shows a balance before the laboratory's own adjustments have been applied, so the amount on the laboratory's statement can be lower.

Patients without insurance

Patients who pay for the test themselves receive a fixed price before testing begins. A financial assistance program lowers the price for households below an income limit. The application form is available from client services and must be received within 90 days of the statement.

Institutional accounts

Hospitals and clinics with an account are invoiced monthly. Questions about an invoice should name the invoice number and the laboratory's client number, not the patient's name.

Contact

The billing office answers questions on weekdays at {{ details.toll_free }}, option 3. Please have the statement at hand. Written questions can be sent to Billing, {{ lab.name }}, {{ details.address }}.
